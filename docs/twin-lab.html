Finding 1 · fidelity

The old twin is close at 2 robots and badly wrong at 4

With your current 2 robots, the original twin over-predicts ABC throughput by about 10%, so the slotting result in the README holds. Each robot you add makes it worse: at 4 robots it over-predicts ABC by 57% and overstates ABC's advantage over random slotting. Both models use identical orders and timings; the only difference is that robots in the new twin are solid and can't share a pod.

Finding 2 · slotting

With more robots, spread the fast movers out, unless you batch

ABC puts the three best sellers in the single closest pod, which turns that pod into a one-at-a-time bottleneck. A new policy, spread:W, deals the fast movers round-robin across the W closest pods. At 3 to 4 robots any spread over 2 to 4 pods helps (the exact best width is a statistical tie), and the original twin can't see any of it: it says spreading always hurts.

It would have told you spreading hurts, the opposite of what solid robots do.

Finding 3 · Experiment 3

When orders trickle in, pool them at the pod

Orders now arrive at random over a 4-hour shift (Poisson, 30-minute warm-up dropped, 10 paired replications per point). Fixed-size and timed batches cut trips but make early orders wait for batch-mates. Pool releases every order immediately, and if a trip to the same pod is already waiting for a robot, the new lines ride along. It batches more as the floor gets busier, with no waiting by design.

Pooling never runs out of robots: a busier floor just means fuller trips. That doesn't make it free. At 600 orders/hr each pod trip serves about 8 orders and the average order waits 8.8 minutes. The useful limit is the service level: 95% of orders within 5 minutes holds up to 270/hr with pooling versus 120/hr without batching. The twin also assumes a bin never runs out of stock, so very large trips are optimistic.

Finding 4 · layout

From the third robot on, move packing off the aisle

The pick point sits on the column-6 tape. While a robot is picking, nothing else can use that stretch of column 6 or wait right beside it. Putting the station in a bay one cell east of the column costs every trip a short detour, but keeps the column open. The trade flips with fleet size: without batching at 3 robots, with batching at 4. The build can be laid out for it now.

For the build

What this changes about the physical warehouse

  1. Build traffic control into the fleet manager from day one. With 2 robots the old twin was about 10% optimistic; by robot 4 it was 57% optimistic. The booking-table planner in warehouse/fleet.py is small enough to run on the laptop that sends MQTT commands, and its plans are exactly what the robots should execute.
  2. Implement pool batching, not fixed batches. On 2 robots it raises the rate at which 95% of orders finish within 5 minutes from 120/hr to 270/hr, it is never slower than no batching, and nobody waits for batch-mates. It is a few lines in the fleet manager: before dispatching a trip, add any new order lines for the same pod.
  3. Keep plain ABC slotting if you batch or pool. Spreading the top sellers across a few pods only pays off when trips are never combined (+4.9% throughput at 4 robots, over 3 pods). With batches of 5, plain ABC wins at every fleet size, and with pooling and 4 robots, spreading was slightly slower (+0.05 ± 0.02 min at 180/hr).
  4. Leave room for a packing bay. With 2 robots the station on the aisle is better (the bay costs 2.6% throughput from the longer detour). From 3 robots on, the bay wins without batching (+5.7% at 4 robots) because column 6 stays open; with batches of 5 it wins only from 4 robots, and with pooling it was the fastest layout at 4 robots (the only fleet size tested with pooling). Build the station so it can slide one cell east when robot 3 (no batching) or robot 4 (batching or pooling) arrives.
  5. Calibrate before trusting any of these numbers. Time robot 1 per cell, per turn, and per lift with the overhead camera, put the values in config.py, and rerun. The pod-bottleneck effect depends on pick and lift times, so its size will move.
  6. Run the physical test that separates the models. 4 robots, ABC, 50 orders: the original twin predicts about 279 orders/hr and the traffic-aware twin about 178. That gap is large enough to see on a tabletop.
Method

How the new twin works and how it was checked

  • Planner. Prioritized space-time A* over (node, heading, time) with a reservation table on 0.4 s ticks. A robot crossing a tape segment books both end nodes for the whole crossing, so robots can't swap places or tailgate inside one cell. The pick point sits on the column-6 tape, so traffic along that column passes through it, and no robot may stand half a cell from a robot that is picking. Finished robots stay booked where they parked. Turn and travel times are the same as config.py.
  • Fleet manager. One robot per pod (pod locking); a trip goes to the robot already parked under that pod if there is one, otherwise the nearest idle robot.
  • Checks. With 1 robot the new twin matches the old one within 0.3%. An independent validator rebuilds every path from the plan log and found 0 collisions, 0 shared pods, 0 station overlaps and 0 clearance violations across 2,605 trips. Mutation tests confirm it catches errors: a planner with collision checks removed produced over 100 detected collisions in one run, and removing pod locking makes the planner stop with a clear deadlock error. A separate code review caught that an earlier version of the planner let robots cut past the pick point while another robot was picking; that is fixed, the validator now checks for it, and every result here was rerun afterwards.
  • Statistics. Common random numbers throughout (every policy sees the same orders at the same times); 95% confidence intervals from paired t-tests.
  • Limits. Robot timings are still the placeholders in config.py; calibrating them with robot 1 remains the most important next step. Robots follow their plans exactly, with no slip or sensor delay.
